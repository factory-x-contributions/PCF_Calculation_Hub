To have an optimal architecture of such mechanisms working in harmony together there are then certain objectives needed to be taken into account. This is because manufacturing environments are diverse both in terms of size and in terms of the level of their modernization. While in many big industrial facilities certain mechanisms are already in place to support various requirements of energy or carbon footprint transparency, in many other ones, especially smaller companies, they are absent and not financially viable to include. A primary example of such module is a manufacturing execution system (MES), which is often absent in many small or medium sized companies. Furthermore, other requirements in terms of energy and carbon footprint transparency render some additional gaps for a few more mechanisms that are essential for efficient storage, aggregation, and post-processing of data for further publication within cross-company layers.In this document, we illustrate two of the applications that were developed within the Factory-X project, to address the above gaps for both small-medium sized companies as well as large manufacturers who still require correct aggregation and calculation of data to be able to publish their product carbon footprint (PCF). This then renders additionally the basis for the use-case demonstration of Factory-X and MX-Port usage within. Namely, IIH-Aggregation-Wizard, serves as a consumption data aggregation mechanism for the shopfloor layer that leverages the ring-buffer storage within industrial edge, while also meeting the north bound requirements of applications that need this aggregated data for further post-processing (e.g., mapping the correct semantics and product information). This is particularly done by the second application, namely PCF-Calculation-Hub, which receives the aggregated data through different interfaces to carry out the complete product carbon footprint and transfer the information to the PCF-Management-Tool (e.g., SiGREEN), for further publications.Product Carbon FootprintA product carbon footprint (PCF) is basically an standardized metric to report the greenhouse gas emissions associated with a product over its considered life cycle or scope—often with emphasis on factory- and process-related emissions (energy, materials, and process inputs) that can be tied to specific work orders, operations, and time windows. Turning shopfloor consumption measurements into such semantics requires more than spreadsheets: it needs consistent measurement, clear allocation rules (for example separating production from idle or non-productive or non-direct energy use), and stable identifiers so results can be audited, compared, and exchanged across suppliers and customers.Catena-XCatena-X and related initiatives address exactly that interoperability challenge of PCF relevant data exchange. In the Catena-X data ecosystem, PCF information is intended to be standardized, shareable, and machine-readable, so an OEM can request a footprint from a supplier and both sides rely on a standardized semantics. While the full Catena-X landscape spans many use cases, the PCF track is concerned with primary operational data, material and process structure (e.g. bill-of-materials and bill-of-process perspectives), and reporting to PCF management systems—so that footprints are not isolated calculations but connected, versionable artifacts in a network of trust. At its technical core, Catena-X builds on a set of open standards and reference implementations maintained under the Eclipse Tractus-X project. Data sovereignty is a first-class concern: every participant retains control over what they share and with whom, enforced through policy-based connectors—most notably the Eclipse Dataspace Connector (EDC)—that negotiate data access contracts before any exchange takes place. This means a Tier-2 supplier can expose its PCF data to a direct customer without inadvertently disclosing it to competitors further up the chain, a property that is essential for gaining industry-wide trust and participation. The semantic foundation for PCF exchange is provided by the Sustainability aspect models defined in Catena-X's BAMM/SAMM-based modelling language. These aspect models precisely specify what a Product Carbon Footprint payload must contain—its system boundaries, allocation rules, activity data, emission factors, and conformance declarations—in a way that is both human-readable and directly consumable by software. By anchoring calculations to a shared ontology, the standard removes the ambiguity that typically arises when companies use different LCA tools, different system boundaries, or different emission factor databases, and makes automated validation and comparison possible. In addition, Catena-X does not operate in isolation. It actively coordinates with parallel initiatives—such as the PACT network, GBA (Global Battery Alliance) data transparency efforts, and the emerging ISO 59040 series—to ensure that the standards converge rather than fragment. The goal is a future in which a PCF data asset created once, in a compliant and sovereign way, can flow across industry ecosystems without manual re-entry, re-validation, or loss of provenance.Yet, while its standardization focuses on interoperable PCF sovereign exchange in the dataspace, it does not address the complete shopfloor-to-calculation pipelines. Manufacturing Execution System (MES) Sitting normally at the heart of a factory, a Manufacturing Execution System (MES) serves as an orchestrator of production within a manufacturing environment.  If an ERP system handles business-level planning—what to produce, in what quantities, for which customers—and the shop floor automation handles the physical act of making things, the MES occupies the layer in between: it takes a production order and translates it into concrete, real-time instructions for people, machines, and materials, while simultaneously collecting data on everything that happens during execution. MES connects machines, operators, and systems to provide actionable insights into how raw materials are transformed into finished goods, bridging the gap between enterprise systems and shop floor operations. Its core functions span the full arc of production. It manages work order dispatching and sequencing, tracks material consumption and batch genealogy, monitors machine states and overall equipment effectiveness (OEE), enforces quality checks at each step, and maintains a complete, time-stamped record of what was produced, by whom, on which equipment, and under which process conditions. This traceability function in an MES allows buyers and the public to expect manufacturing companies to document the location, time and method of processing materials, parts, and end products with complete transparency. In regulated industries this is a compliance requirement; in the context of product-level carbon accounting, it is the prerequisite for any credible PCF calculation. This implies that MES also holds exactly the primary data that a PCF calculation requires. That is how much energy a specific part consumed during machining, which material batch was used and at what yield. This same data foundation enables a more forward-looking capability. This means we can transform carbon from a lagging indicator into a controllable variable. Instead of calculating a product's footprint after the fact, an MES with sustainability integration can optimize and suggest production scheduling decisions in real time.
